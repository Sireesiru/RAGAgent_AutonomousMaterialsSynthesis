QUESTION:
What are the specific limitations of electron microscopy and fluorescence microscopy when used to study cell development?

REFERENCE / GOLD ANSWER:
Electron microscopy can disclose structural layout and macromolecular arrangement but cannot directly follow dynamics in living cells; fluorescence microscopy can follow dynamics but requires labeling and lacks spatial resolution.

GEMMA-ONLY ANSWER:
The specific limitations of electron microscopy (EM) and fluorescence microscopy in studying cell development are as follows:

**Electron Microscopy (EM)**
*   **Sample Preparation:** EM requires extensive sample fixation, dehydration, and often heavy-metal staining or resin embedding. This process can introduce artifacts and may alter the native state of the cell.
*   **Static Imaging:** EM provides a "snapshot" in time. It cannot capture dynamic processes or real-time movements of organelles or proteins during development.
*   **Sample Viability:** The preparation process typically kills the cells, making it impossible to observe live biological processes.
*   **Field of View:** The high magnification required for high resolution results in a very small field of view, making it difficult to observe large-scale tissue organization or systemic developmental changes.

**Fluorescence Microscopy**
*   **Phototoxicity and Photobleaching:** High-intensity light can damage living cells (phototoxicity) or cause the fluorescent markers to fade (photobleaching), limiting the duration of live-cell imaging.
*   **Specificity and Labeling:** Accuracy depends on the specificity of the fluorophores. Non-specific binding or the requirement for genetic modification (e.g., GFP tagging) can alter the behavior or expression of the proteins being studied.
*   **Resolution Limits:** Standard fluorescence microscopy is limited by the diffraction limit of light (approximately 200–300 nm), which may be insufficient to resolve small sub-cellular structures compared to EM.
*   **Autofluorescence:** Natural molecules in some tissues can emit light, potentially interfering with the signal from the intended fluorophores.
